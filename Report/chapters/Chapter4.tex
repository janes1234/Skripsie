
\mychapter{Dataset}{Dataset}{}\label{chap:systemmethods}

The dataset used in this project consists of satellite imagery sourced from Google Earth and covers five waste water treatment works (WWTW) facilities: Atlantis, Cape Flats, Waterval, Fisantekraal and Northern Works. Each image is a snapshot of a facility's biological reactors at a particular point in time, and its filename encodes the month and year in which it was captured, although the exact format differs between facilities (for example \texttt{Atlantis 01\_2019}, \texttt{capeflats1\_201408} and \texttt{Northern Works 2020 05}).

As seen in Figure \ref{fig:heatmap}, most of them (423 of the 620) were captured between 2016 and 2022, and they are spread fairly evenly over the months of the year, with between 38 (November) and 62 (August) images per month.

\section{Annotations}

Images were manually annotated using the VGG Image Annotator (VIA2) tool \citep{dutta2019vgg}. Aerobic zones were labelled using rectangular bounding boxes, while clarifiers were labelled using circular regions, reflecting the physical shape of each structure. Each annotation carries a region attribute indicating the visible state of that component at the time the image was captured.

Aerobic zones were labelled into one of three categories, based on how much of the surface exhibits the characteristic "leopard print" aeration pattern. Examples are shown in Figure \ref{fig:aerobic_examples}: \textbf{Functional}: the surface shows the leopard print pattern. \textbf{Suboptimal}: a third or more of the surface shows no leopard print, or the state is unclear. \textbf{Dysfunctional}: two thirds or more of the surface shows no leopard print, or is clearly dysfunctional.

Clarifiers were labelled into one of four categories, based on surface colour and visibility. Examples are shown in Figure \ref{fig:clarifier_examples}: \textbf{Functional}: surface appears uniformly black. \textbf{Dysfunctional}: surface appears green. \textbf{Scum}: surface is not uniformly black. \textbf{Empty}: shadows are visible inside the tank.

Annotations are stored as VIA2 project files in JSON format. Each entry records the image filename, file size, and a list of regions, where each region specifies its shape (rectangle or circle), pixel coordinates, and the associated class label. An example entry for an aerobic zone annotation is given in Appendix~\ref{appen:dataset_details}.

\section{Facility Structure}

The facilities differ slightly in how their data is organised, reflecting differences in plant layout. Atlantis, Fisantekraal and Northern Works each consist of a single reactor unit, with all images stored in one folder. Cape Flats consists of four units (CF1--CF4) and Waterval of two (Top and Bottom), each with its own image folder. Every unit has its own aerobic zone and clarifier label file, except at Fisantekraal and Northern Works, where only clarifiers were labelled. \section{Data Preparation}\label{sec:pipeline}

Before the labelled regions can be used to train a classifier, they need to be extracted from the full facility images and organised by class. This is handled by a preparation script (\texttt{prep.py}), which reads the VIA2 annotation files, crops out each labelled region, and saves the crops into a folder structure that can be loaded directly with \texttt{torchvision.datasets.ImageFolder}.

The script processes each facility one unit at a time and works through the aerobic zone and clarifier label files separately. For each labelled region, it converts the shape (a rectangle for an aerobic zone, a circle for a clarifier) to a bounding box, clamped to the image edges in case a region was drawn slightly outside the image. The class label is normalised to remove capitalisation inconsistencies and checked against the expected classes for that component. For a clarifier, every pixel outside the annotated circle is set to black, so that only the tank remains visible. The region is then cropped and saved as a JPG file in the folder matching its class label.

Each output filename encodes the facility, unit, and original image name, so that a crop can always be traced back to the source image it came from. For example, a crop from unit CF2 at Cape Flats would be saved with a filename beginning \texttt{CapeFlats\_Images-CF2\_...}, followed by the original image name and a region number.

The result is a folder structure with one top-level folder per component and one subfolder per class:

{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
cnn_dataset/
    aerobic_zone/
        Dysfunctional/
        Functional/
        Suboptimal/
    clarifier/
        Dysfunctional/
        Empty/
        Functional/
        Scum/
\end{verbatim}
}

The two classes that are excluded from the dataset (Section~\ref{sec:excluded}) have no folder. This structure means the aerobic zone and clarifier sub-images are treated as two separate classification problems, each with its own set of class folders, rather than a single combined dataset.


\section{Excluded from the dataset}\label{sec:excluded}
Two annotated classes had too few examples to be used: a single aerobic zone labelled \textbf{Empty} and four clarifiers labelled \textbf{Stagnant}. Both were excluded, since so few examples cannot be split into training, development and test sets, and would not allow the CNN to learn a reliable representation of the class.


\section{Data Splitting}
In the following section the way the available data is split into training, development and testing partitions is described.

\subsection{Leave-one-out Split}

To evaluate how well the trained classifier generalises to WWTW facilities it has never encountered, the dataset was partitioned using a leave-one-out strategy rather than a random split. One facility, chosen from the five available (Atlantis, Cape Flats, Waterval, Fisantekraal, and Northern Works), was the test facility and withheld from training entirely: none of its images appear in the training or development sets, and it plays no part in training. Note that this is repeated once for each facility: five times for the clarifiers, and three times for the aerobic zones, which are only labelled at Atlantis, Cape Flats and Waterval. This guarantees that test performance reflects the model's ability to classify components at an unseen site.

This split was applied independently for each facility component, aerobic zone and clarifier, since the two have different classes and different image sets.

Once the test facility was removed, the remaining four facilities' images were divided into training and development sets. A random split at this stage risks an uneven mix: the development set could end up dominated by whichever facility or unit happened to contribute the most images, or by images from a narrow range of capture dates, which would give an unrepresentative estimate of development performance.

To avoid this, images were first grouped by facility, unit and class (some facilities have more than one unit, for example Cape Flats has 4 units each with its own set of images). Different units at the same facility can differ in lighting, or capture date, so treating them as separate groups during shuffling keeps that variation spread across both training and development rather than concentrated in one. Within each group, images were shuffled using a fixed random seed to mix capture dates before being partitioned according to a fixed development ratio (20\%). Splitting within these smaller groups rather
than across the whole set ensures that every facility, unit, and class combination among the four training facilities is represented in both
the training and development sets, in proportion to how much data it contributes. Figure~\ref{fig:leave_one_out approach} in Appendix~\ref{appen:dataset_details} shows the resulting split for the clarifiers when Waterval is withheld as the test facility.


\subsection{Chronological Split}
In addition to the leave-one-out approach described above, a chronological split was used to produce training, development, and test sets directly from the dataset, without holding any facility out. This split is made by capture date: every image captured from January 2020 onwards is a test image, and the images captured before January 2020 are divided into training and development sets in an 80/20 ratio, using the same facility, unit, class grouping and shuffling procedure described in the previous section. The split is made by date rather than at random for two reasons. First, the system would classify images that are newer than every image it was trained on. Second, a random split of the cropped images would put crops from the same satellite image, captured on the same date and in the same conditions, into both the training and the test set. The cut-off of January 2020 was chosen so that the test set of every facility contains every class that occurs at that facility. As a result, all 3 \textbf{Empty} clarifiers at Atlantis and all 7 \textbf{Dysfunctional} clarifiers at Waterval are in the test set. The resulting number of images per class is given in Section~\ref{sec:exp_mixed}.

\section{Relabelling}

The dataset is new and was prepared shortly before the project started by annotators under the supervision of Prof F. de Wet. Early inspection found occasional labelling errors, so every clarifier image was reviewed manually with a relabelling notebook built on Jupyter widgets. The full set was reviewed, not just the images the classifier got wrong, because reviewing only those could introduce bias. The notebook shows each image with its current label and a button for each class. Corrections are written to a separate \texttt{relabel/} copy of the annotation files, so the original VIA2 labels stay intact.

Figure \ref{fig:relabel_example} shows a Cape Flats clarifier that was relabelled from \textbf{Scum} to \textbf{Empty}. The tank appears to have been drained: its floor is visible, and the dark band across it is the bridge's shadow, not scum. The review interface is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:relabel_process_example} in Appendix~\ref{appen:dataset_details}.

\caption{A relabelled clarifier from Cape Flats, shown with some of its surroundings. The original label was ``Scum'', but the label was corrected to ``Empty'', since the tank appears to be drained, with its floor and the shadow of the bridge visible inside it.}

The review proposed 293 corrections, and the supervisor checked each one. 166 were accepted, 99 were rejected, and 28 were given a different label, so 194 regions ended up with a label different from the original. A script applied these decisions to the \texttt{relabel/} copy. It first checked that each region still held the proposed label, and it logged every decision. These labels are used in every experiment in Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_design}.

\section{Final Dataset}\label{sec:final_dataset}

Tables~\ref{tab:aerobic_summary} and~\ref{tab:clarifier_summary} give the final number of images per class, per unit, after relabelling. These are the counts used in every experiment. The aerobic zone dataset contains 1743 images, all from Atlantis, Cape Flats and Waterval, and the clarifier dataset contains 2527 images from all five facilities. Both datasets are imbalanced. Suboptimal is the smallest aerobic zone class with 223 images, against 813 for Dysfunctional. Among the clarifiers, Empty has only 145 images, against 1087 for Scum. The classes are also spread unevenly across facilities: Waterval Top accounts for 344 of the 707 Functional aerobic zones, and Northern Works accounts for 131 of the 292 Dysfunctional clarifiers. Section~\ref{sec:exp_loss} describes how this imbalance is handled during training.
