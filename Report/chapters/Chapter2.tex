\section{Wastewater Treatment Works}
Municipal wastewater treatment plants purify our water using a series of steps. Large facilities typically rely on a biological method known as the activated sludge process. This process utilises enormous tanks filled with beneficial microorganisms that naturally break down pollutants. During this process, the microorganisms convert the organic pollutants primarily into carbon dioxide, water, and new cellular biomass. These bacteria require a steady supply of oxygen to survive and work efficiently. To provide this air, facilities often use a technique called Fine Bubble Diffused Aeration (FBDA), which continuously pumps millions of tiny bubbles directly into the water. This happens in a part of the treatment plant known as \textbf{aerobic zones}.

Once this active treatment is complete, the mixture flows into large circular settling tanks called \textbf{clarifiers}. The dense microbial biomass gradually settles to the bottom of the tank. This settling process leaves clear, treated water at the surface. Rather than discarding this settled biomass, the system continuously circulates it back to the initial tanks. Returning this activated sludge maintains a sufficient concentration of active microorganisms, ensuring the system can efficiently metabolise incoming organic matter.

Interestingly, it is possible to tell a lot about how well these systems are performing just from aerial or satellite photos. A healthy FBDA unit churns the surface into a ``leopard print'' pattern, while a struggling one looks noticeably smoother. Well functioning clarifiers separate out the solids and leave behind a clear liquid that looks black from above. When they are operating poorly, solids escape into the effluent and the surface turns green, cloudy, or uneven. Shown in Figure~\ref{fig:treatment_comparison} are examples of functional and dysfunctional aerators and clarifiers, highlighting the visual cues that indicate their performance levels.

\section{Convolutional Neural Networks}\label{sec:cnn_background}

\subsection{Building Blocks of a Convolutional Neural Network}\label{sec:cnn_building_blocks}

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) \citep{lecun1998gradient} is a type of artificial neural network designed for data with a grid-like structure, such as the pixel grid of an image. A CNN slides a small filter, or \textbf{kernel}, across the image, reusing the same weights at every position. This weight sharing keeps the number of learnable parameters low even for large images, and reflects a reasonable assumption about images of physical objects: a pattern that is useful for identifying, say, the rippled surface of a functioning aerator is just as useful whether it appears in the top-left or bottom-right corner of the image. This makes CNNs particularly well suited to the classification task in this project, where the relevant visual cues, such as the ``leopard print'' texture of a functional aerator or the colour and clarity of a clarifier's surface, can appear anywhere within an image and at different scales.

Formally, given an input feature map $X$ with $C_{in}$ channels and a kernel $K$ of size $k \times k$, a convolutional layer computes an output feature map $Y$ where each output value is a weighted sum of a small $k \times k$ neighbourhood of the input, taken across all input channels:
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,j,m} = b_m + \sum_{u=0}^{k-1}\sum_{v=0}^{k-1}\sum_{c=1}^{C_{in}} K_{u,v,c,m}\,X_{i+u,\,j+v,\,c}
\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $i, j$ index the spatial position (row, column) of the output value being computed;
    \item $m$ indexes the output channel, i.e. which of the $C_{out}$ kernels produced this value;
    \item $u, v$ index the position within the $k \times k$ kernel window, ranging over the neighbourhood being summed;
    \item $c$ indexes the input channel being summed over;
    \item $b_m$ is a learnable bias term, one per output channel.
\end{itemize}
In words, $Y_{i,j,m}$ is obtained by placing the kernel so its top-left corner is located at position $(i,j)$ in $X$, multiplying it element-wise against every input channel underneath it, and summing the result. Each of the $C_{out}$ kernels applied at a layer produces one such output channel, also called a \textbf{feature map}, so a convolutional layer with $C_{out}$ kernels turns a $C_{in}$-channel input into a $C_{out}$-channel output.

Because the same kernel weights are reused at every position $(i,j)$, a single convolutional layer only ever looks at a $k \times k$ patch of its input. Depth is what lets the network see further: stacking layers causes the \textbf{receptive field} (the area of the original image able to influence a given output value) to grow, since a unit in the second layer combines several first-layer outputs, each of which already summarised a $k \times k$ patch of the original image. Two stacked $k \times k$ convolutions therefore give a receptive field of $(2k-1)\times(2k-1)$ pixels, even though neither individual kernel looks beyond its own $k \times k$ window. This is what allows a CNN to build up from simple, local features such as edges and colour gradients in its early layers to increasingly large-scale and abstract patterns, such as the overall texture or shape of a component, in its deeper layers.

Convolutions are usually applied with a \textbf{stride} $s$ (the step size the kernel moves between applications) and zero-\textbf{padding} $p$ (extra rows and columns of zeros added around the input border, used to maintain the input-output dimensions of the convolution). For a square input of dimensions $W \times W$, the resulting output dimension is
\begin{equation}
O = \left\lfloor \frac{W - k + 2p}{s} \right\rfloor + 1,
\label{eq:conv_output_size}
\end{equation}
where $O$ is the resulting width and height of the output feature map such that the output feature map has dimensions $O \times O$. A stride greater than one causes the filter to take larger steps accross the input feature map, which shrinks the output feature map and reduces computional work.

After each convolution, an element-wise, non-linear \textbf{activation function} is applied to every value of $Y$. This allows it to learn more complex patterns in the data. All of the architectures used in this project use the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU), or a smooth variant of it:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x),
\label{eq:relu}
\end{equation}
which simply passes positive input values through unchanged while producing a zero for negative inputs.

\textbf{Pooling} layers are used to progressively reduce the spatial size of the feature maps, most commonly by taking the maximum value within a small patch:
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,j} = \max_{0 \le u,v < k} X_{i \cdot s + u,\ j \cdot s + v},
\label{eq:maxpool}
\end{equation}
where $i,j$ again index the output position, and $u,v$ index the $k \times k$ pooling window itself, now taken over a single channel at a time rather than summed across channels as in Equation~\ref{eq:convolution}. Applied with a stride $s$ equal to the window size $k$, this reduces the resolution of the feature map, reducing the computation required by later layers while making the resulting representation somewhat more robust to small translations of the input. For this project, this means a feature like the ``leopard print'' texture produces roughly the same pooled values even when it is located a few pixels off from where it was in another image.

By repeating convolutional, activation, and pooling layers, a CNN gradually trades spatial resolution for channel depth: early feature maps are large and shallow, and later ones are small and deep. Once the feature map is small enough, it is flattened, or globally pooled, into a single feature vector and passed through one or more fully-connected layers, ending in an output layer with one unit per class. A \textbf{softmax} function then converts these raw scores, or \textbf{logits} $z_k$, into a probability distribution over the $K$ possible classes:
\begin{equation}
p_k = \frac{\exp(z_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(z_j)}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K,
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
where $z_k$ is the logit for class $k$, $p_k$ is its resulting probability, and the sum in the denominator is over all $K$ classes so that the resulting values of $p_k$ sum to one. The classification decision is the class assigned the highest probability, and the network is trained by adjusting every learnable kernel and weight to make $p_k$ as close as possible to 1 for the correct class, most commonly via the cross-entropy loss.

Training a CNN's convolutional layers entirely from random weight initialisations typically requires very large labelled datasets, since the network has to learn everything from edge detection upward using only the available training images. This project instead relies on \textbf{transfer learning}: each architecture is first pretrained on ImageNet \citep{deng2009imagenet, russakovsky2015imagenet}, a benchmark dataset consisting of over a million labelled natural images spanning a thousand object classes, so that its convolutional layers already encode a broad range of general-purpose visual features before ever seeing a wastewater treatment image. Its final classification layer is then removed and replaced with a small classifier head fitted to the classes used in this project, and the network is fine-tuned on the WWTW image dataset. Four such pretrained architectures, all originally developed for the ImageNet benchmark, were evaluated in this project: \textbf{ResNet-18} \citep{he2016resnet}, \textbf{DenseNet-121} \citep{huang2017densenet}, \textbf{EfficientNet-B0} \citep{tan2019efficientnet}, and \textbf{ConvNeXt-Tiny} \citep{liu2022convnext}. ResNet-18 is also trained a second time from randomly initialised weights, as a baseline that measures the effect of pretraining (Section~\ref{sec:exp_architectures}). Each combines the convolutional building blocks above in a different way, as described in the remainder of this section.

\subsection{Residual Networks: ResNet-18}
\label{sec:resnet}

Residual Networks (ResNet) \citep{he2016resnet} are built from \textbf{residual blocks}. Instead of learning a completely new mapping, the layers of a block learn a residual $F(x)$, and a \textbf{shortcut}, or \textbf{skip}, connection bypasses the input $x$ around the layers and adds it to their output:
\begin{equation}
y = F(x) + x.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
In traditional networks, the gradient weakens exponentially as it backproagates through each layer, making it difficult to train very deep networks. The skip connection allows the gradient to flow to earlier layers during backpropagation.

ResNet-18 uses a \textbf{basic block} of two $3\times3$ convolutions, each followed by batch normalisation and ReLU, except that the second ReLU is applied after the shortcut is added. Deeper variants such as ResNet-50 use a \textbf{bottleneck block}, in which a $1\times1$ convolution reduces the number of channels, a $3\times3$ convolution operates on the reduced representation, and a second $1\times1$ convolution restores the channel count.

Table~\ref{tab:resnet_layers} lists the layers of ResNet-18. The 18 counts the layers with weights: 17 convolutional layers and one fully-connected layer. The first convolution of each of the last three stages has a stride of 2, which halves the resolution, while the number of kernels doubles. This means that $x$ and $F(x)$ then no longer have the same shape, so a $1\times1$ convolution with stride 2, followed by batch normalisation, is applied on the shortcut to make the shapes match for the element-wise addition.

\subsection{Densely Connected Networks: DenseNet-121}\label{sec:densenet}

DenseNet \citep{huang2017densenet} extends the shortcut idea of ResNet. Within a \textbf{dense block}, each layer receives the feature maps of all preceding layers in the block, concatenated along the channel dimension:
\begin{equation}
x_l = H_l\big([x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{l-1}]\big),
\label{eq:densenet}
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is the input to the block and $x_l$ the output of layer $l$. Because the inputs are concatenated rather than added as in Equation~\ref{eq:residual}, each layer contributes $k$ new channels to the block, where $k$ is called the \textbf{growth rate}. If the input $x_0$ to a block has $k_0$ channels, the input to layer $l$ therefore has $k_0 + k(l-1)$ channels. We call each such layer a \textbf{dense layer}. In DenseNet-121, $k=32$ and $H_l$ consists of two convolutions, each preceded by batch normalisation and a ReLU: a $1\times1$ convolution that produces $4k$ channels, followed by a $3\times3$ convolution that produces the $k$ new channels.

Between dense blocks, a \textbf{transition layer} halves the number of channels with a $1\times1$ convolution and halves the resolution with a $2\times2$ average pool. DenseNet-121 has four dense blocks with 6, 12, 24 and 16 dense layers respectively (Table~\ref{tab:densenet_layers}). Each dense layer contains two convolutions, so the blocks contribute $2(6+12+24+16) = 116$ convolutions. The initial convolution, the three transition convolutions and the fully-connected layer bring the total to the 121 layers. Because each layer can reuse the features of all earlier layers in its block, every layer can be narrow. The network has 8.0M parameters, fewer than the 11.7M of ResNet-18.

\subsection{Efficient Networks: EfficientNet-B0}\label{sec:efficientnet}

EfficientNet \citep{tan2019efficientnet} is built using \textbf{mobile inverted bottleneck convolution} (MBConv) blocks, which split a full convolution into cheaper steps. First, a $1\times1$ convolution increases the number of channels by a factor $t$. Second, a $k\times k$ \textbf{depthwise} convolution looks for spatial patterns, such as edges and texture, in each channel separately. Because it does not combine channels, it needs $C$ times fewer multiply-adds than a full convolution with $C$ input and $C$ output channels (Equation~\ref{eq:convolution}). Third, a \textbf{squeeze-and-excitation} block \citep{hu2018squeeze} weights each channel by its importance for the current image. To compute this importance, the average of the feature map in each channel is computed and passed through two small fully-connected layers, the second of which ends in a sigmoid. The resulting outputs, which lie between 0 and 1, are viewed as importance values and are used to multiplicatively scale the feature map of each channel. Finally, a $1\times1$ convolution combines the channels and reduces their number again. The depthwise convolution therefore finds spatial patterns, while the $1\times1$ convolutions mix the channels. When the input and output of a block have the same shape, the input is added to the output through a residual connection, as in ResNet (Equation~\ref{eq:residual}). The activation is SiLU, $\mathrm{SiLU}(x) = x\sigma(x)$, where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, which is a smooth variant of ReLU.

\subsection{ConvNeXt-Tiny}\label{sec:convnext}

ConvNeXt \citep{liu2022convnext} starts from a standard ResNet-50 and changes it step by step to resemble a Vision Transformer, a newer type of network that splits the image into small patches, while still using only convolutions. The ConvNeXt block has three steps. First, a $7\times7$ depthwise convolution looks for spatial patterns in each channel separately. The large kernel lets the block see a wider area of the image, and because the convolution is depthwise, it remains cheap. Second, a $1\times1$ convolution increases the number of channels by a factor of 4, and is followed by the GELU activation. Third, a second $1\times1$ convolution reduces the number of channels back to the original number. The input of the block is then added to its output through a residual connection, as in ResNet (Equation~\ref{eq:residual}). The block is therefore narrow at its ends and wide in the middle, which is the opposite of the ResNet bottleneck block (Section~\ref{sec:resnet}) but similar to MBConv (Section~\ref{sec:efficientnet}). The block uses \textbf{layer normalisation}, which normalises over the channels of each image instead of over a batch of images, and has only one normalisation and one activation, where the ResNet bottleneck block has three of each. GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) is a smooth variant of ReLU \citep{hendrycks2016gelu}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{GELU}(x) = x \cdot \Phi(x),
\label{eq:gelu}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. At the input, the $7\times7$ convolution and max pool of ResNet are replaced by a $4\times4$ convolution with stride 4, called a \textbf{patchify} layer, which divides the image into non-overlapping $4\times4$ patches. Between stages, the resolution is halved by a separate $2\times2$ convolution with stride 2. \section{Evaluation Metrics for Classification}\label{sec:metrics_background}

The performance of a classifier can be measured in several ways.

\textbf{Accuracy} is the fraction of test images that are assigned to their true class. When the classes are imbalanced, it is dominated by the largest classes.

\textbf{Precision, recall and F1 score} are calculated for each class $c$ from the number of true positives $TP_c$, false positives $FP_c$ and false negatives $FN_c$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Precision}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c}, \qquad
    \mathrm{Recall}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c}, \qquad
    F1_c = \frac{2 \cdot \mathrm{Precision}_c \cdot \mathrm{Recall}_c}{\mathrm{Precision}_c + \mathrm{Recall}_c}.
\end{equation}
The macro-averaged values are the unweighted means over the classes, so that every class has the same weight. The \textbf{confusion matrix} counts, for every true class, how many images were assigned to each class. It therefore reflects classes that are often confused. 

The \textbf{receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve} plots the true positive rate (recall) against the false positive rate as the decision threshold is varied. The \textbf{area under the curve (AUC)} is 1 when every positive image receives a higher score than every negative image, and 0.5 for random guessing. For more than two classes, the one-vs-rest AUC of each class can be averaged.

\textbf{Loss and accuracy curves} give the training and development loss and accuracy after every epoch.


\section{Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping}\label{sec:gradcam_background}

Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)~\citep{selvaraju2017gradcam} produces a heatmap that shows which regions of an input image contributed the most to the output of a CNN for a given class.

Grad-CAM uses the output of the last convolutional layer, which is the layer before the global pooling and the fully connected classification layers. This output consists of one feature map per channel.Let $A^k$ denote the feature map of channel $k$, $A^k_{ij}$ the element in row $i$ and column $j$. A weight $\alpha_k^c$ is calculated for each channel by taking the gradient of the score $y^c$ of class $c$, which is the logit of that class before the softmax (Equation~\ref{eq:softmax}), with respect to every element of $A^k$, and averaging these gradients over the feature map:

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_k^c = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{i} \sum_{j} \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}},
    \label{eq:gradcam_weights}
\end{equation}

where the sums run over all rows $i$ and columns $j$ of the feature map, and $Z$ is the number of elements in the feature map (its height multiplied by its width).

The feature maps of all channels are then multiplied by their weights and summed.Finally only the positive elements of this matrix are kept:

\begin{equation}
    L^c = \mathrm{ReLU}\left( \sum_{k} \alpha_k^c A^k \right).
    \label{eq:gradcam_map}
\end{equation}

The map $L^c$ has the same height and width as the feature maps of the last convolutional layer, and is much smaller than the input image. It is therefore resized to the dimensions of the input image, normalised to the range $[0, 1]$, and superimposed on the image as a heatmap.

Because the weights $\alpha_k^c$ depend on $c$, a separate heatmap is obtained for each class $c = 1, \dots, K$ from the same image. The heatmap of class $c$ highlights the regions whose features increase the score $y^c$. Regions that lower this score are set to zero by the ReLU. Comparing the heatmaps of different classes therefore shows which parts of the image the network associates with each class, and whether a prediction is based on the relevant object or on unrelated parts of the image.
