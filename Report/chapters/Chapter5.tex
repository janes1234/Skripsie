\section{Overview}
\label{sec:exp_overview}

This chapter describes the classification system and the experiments performed with it. The clarifiers and the aerobic zones are separate classification problems (Chapter~\ref{chap:systemmethods}) but use the same classifier architecture, and every experiment is run for both unless stated otherwise. Sections~\ref{sec:exp_architectures} to~\ref{sec:exp_training} describe the shared parts of the system: the architectures and transfer learning, augmentation, class imbalance, hyperparameter tuning and the training loop. Three experiments are then performed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mixed-facility baseline} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_mixed}): all five facilities are pooled and split chronologically. Images captured from January 2020 onwards are test images.
    \item \textbf{Leave-one-facility-out} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_lofo}): each facility is held out in turn as the test set. This gives five held-out test sets for the clarifiers and three for the aerobic zones, and estimates performance on an unseen facility.
    \item \textbf{Binary (OK / Not-OK)} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_binary}): \textbf{Functional} is OK and all other classes are Not-OK.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:exp_metrics} describes the evaluation metrics and Section~\ref{sec:exp_gradcam} the Grad-CAM method. Figure~\ref{fig:experimental_pipeline} shows the experimental procedure. Each experiment consists of one architecture trained for one component on one data split, saves its test metrics, confusion matrix, training history and per-image predictions to a \texttt{results\_*.pkl} file.

\section{Model Architectures and Transfer Learning}\label{sec:exp_architectures}

Four CNN architectures are compared in this project: ResNet-18 (Section~\ref{sec:resnet}), DenseNet-121 (Section~\ref{sec:densenet}), EfficientNet-B0 (Section~\ref{sec:efficientnet}) and ConvNeXt-Tiny (Section~\ref{sec:convnext}). ResNet-18 is evaluated in two ways: once fine-tuned from ImageNet weights and once from randomly initialised weights. Therefore, in total, five models are evaluated. Table~\ref{tab:arch_sizes} lists the sizes of the architectures, which range from 5.3 to 28.6 million parameters. All four are taken from the torchvision library \citep{torchvision2016}, and the same five models are trained for both the clarifiers and the aerobic zones.

The ResNet-50~\citep{he2016resnet} and Inception-v3~\citep{szegedy2016inception} architectures were also evaluated in this work. However, neither had the highest macro recall or mean AUC for either component in either experiment. Due to space restrictions, they are therefore not reported on.

The ResNet-18 trained from randomly initialised weights is referred to as ResNet-18 (scratch). It is tuned in the same way as the pretrained models (Section~\ref{sec:exp_hyperparams}). It was added to the project to assess the benefit of pretraining, that is, whether the pretrained architectures outperform the same architecture trained only on this project's own, small dataset.

For each of the four pretrained architectures, transfer learning follows the same procedure. The architecture's convolutional backbone is loaded with weights pretrained on ImageNet~\citep{deng2009imagenet, russakovsky2015imagenet}, specifically torchvision's \texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V1} weights. The original 1000-class ImageNet output layer is then discarded, and a new classifier head, is put in its place: a stack of $H$ \texttt{Linear+ReLU} pairs followed by a final \texttt{Linear} layer mapping to the project's number of classes. The dimensions of this head (number of hidden layers $H$ and neurons $N$) are hyperparameters that are optimised (Section~\ref{sec:exp_hyperparams}). Every parameter in the network, backbone included, is then fine-tuned on the WWTW dataset.

As a design choice, ConvNeXt-Tiny's global average pooling layer is replaced with global max pooling, so the feature vector reaching its classifier head is composed of each channel's strongest activation rather than its average. The other architectures keep their default pooling.

Every image is scaled to fit the input size without changing its aspect ratio, and then padded with zeros to the full input size, with the image centred. The input size is $224\times224$ pixels for the clarifiers and $112\times224$ pixels for the aerobic zones. Every image is then normalised with the per-channel ImageNet mean and standard deviation since the pretrained backbones were trained on inputs normalised this way.


\section{Augmentation}
\label{sec:exp_augmentation}

Augmentation is applied to the training images only, by PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} each time an image is loaded, so every epoch sees a new random version of each image. The development and test images are not augmented. Three augmentations are applied, in this order:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Horizontal flip} with a probability of 50\%.
    \item \textbf{Rotation} by a random angle between $-15^\circ$ and $+15^\circ$. The image keeps its size, so corners are cut off and the empty corners are filled with black.
    \item \textbf{Colour jitter}: brightness and contrast are multiplied by a random factor in $[0.6, 1.4]$, saturation by a factor in $[0.7, 1.3]$, and the hue is shifted by up to $\pm 0.1$ of the colour wheel.
\end{itemize}

The images are taken from directly above, so a flipped or rotated clarifier or aerobic zone is still a realistic example of the same class. The black corners from rotation match the black mask that \texttt{prep.py} applies outside each clarifier and the padding added to the aerobic zones (Section~\ref{sec:exp_architectures}). The colour jitter accounts for differences in lighting, sun glare and water colour between facilities and capture dates. Figure~\ref{fig:augmentation_examples} shows the augmentations applied to one training image.

\section{Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:exp_loss}

As shown in Tables~\ref{tab:aerobic_summary} and~\ref{tab:clarifier_summary} in Section~\ref{sec:final_dataset}, the classes of both components are highly imbalanced. For the clarifiers, \textbf{Functional} and \textbf{Scum} clarifiers make up most of the dataset (2090 of the 2527 images), while \textbf{Dysfunctional} and especially \textbf{Empty} clarifiers are much less common. For the aerobic zones, \textbf{Suboptimal} is the rare class, with only 223 of the 1743 images. If every image contributes equally to the loss, the network can reach a low loss by mostly assigning images to the common classes, and has little incentive to learn the rare ones.

To counter this, the cross-entropy loss is weighted per class, so that the network is penalised more for misclassifying an image from a rare class than from a common one. The weight of each class is inversely proportional to the number of training images in that class, and the weights are then normalised so that they sum to the number of classes. For $K$ classes, where $n_c$ is the number of training images in class $c$, the weight of class $c$ is

\begin{equation}
    w_c = K \cdot \frac{1/n_c}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} 1/n_j}.
\end{equation}

A class with average representation therefore gets a weight close to one, a rare class gets a weight larger than one, and a common class gets a weight smaller than one. These weights are passed to PyTorch's \texttt{CrossEntropyLoss}, which multiplies the loss of each image by the weight of its true class, and divides the sum over a batch by the sum of these weights rather than by the number of images. The training data itself is not resampled, so every training image is still used exactly once per epoch.

The weights are calculated from the training set only, and are recalculated for every split, since the class counts change depending on which facility is held out. The held-out facility can change the weights considerably. Cape Flats contains 120 of the 145 \textbf{Empty} clarifiers, so when it is held out, only 21 \textbf{Empty} images remain for training and the \textbf{Empty} weight rises to 3.09, about twenty times that of \textbf{Scum}. Waterval contains 543 of the 707 \textbf{Functional} aerobic zones, so when it is held out, \textbf{Functional} becomes a minority class in training (weight 1.19, compared with 0.34 to 0.68 in the other splits), while it dominates the test set.

The development loss uses the same weights, so the weighting also affects early stopping and checkpoint selection (Section~\ref{sec:exp_training}).


\section{Hyperparameter Tuning}
\label{sec:exp_hyperparams}

Some hyperparameters were fixed for every architecture. These include Adam's $\beta_2$ ($0.999$, the default according to the original paper. \citep{kingma2015adam}), the $L_2$ weight decay ($10^{-4}$), a maximum of 100 epochs, an early-stopping patience of 10 epochs and a random seed of 42. The remaining hyperparameters, namely the number of hidden layers $H$ and neurons per layer $N$ of the classifier head, the learning rate, Adam's $\beta_1$, the \texttt{StepLR} step size and the batch size, were tuned with Optuna~\citep{akiba2019optuna}, a hyperparameter optimisation library. This was done for all five models, separately for the clarifiers and the aerobic zones, giving ten searches.

\subsection{Why Optuna}
\label{subsec:why_optuna}

The six tuned hyperparameters interact, so tuning them one at a time can miss good combinations, and a grid search over all six is too expensive. Optuna searches all six jointly using a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler \citep{bergstra2011tpe}, which samples new candidates from the regions of the search space where earlier trials scored well. A \texttt{MedianPruner} also stops a trial early if its development accuracy falls below the median of the earlier trials at the same epoch.

\subsection{Search Procedure}
\label{subsec:search_procedure}

The first ten trials of every search are sampled at random, and the last ten are guided by TPE. The pruner only becomes active after five trials have completed, and does not prune a trial before its fifth epoch. Every trial uses the same training loop as the final models (Section~\ref{sec:exp_training}) and is scored by its development accuracy at the epoch with the lowest development loss, which is the checkpoint that is kept when a model is trained.

To limit the computational cost, each search was run once rather than for every held-out facility: on the leave-one-facility-out split with Northern Works held out for the clarifiers, and with Atlantis held out for the aerobic zones, since Northern Works has no aerobic zones. The selected values are used for every held-out facility and for the chronological split (Sections~\ref{sec:exp_mixed} and~\ref{sec:exp_lofo}). It is possible that better performance can be achieved by more complete optimisation, but time did not permit this to be investigated.

\section{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:exp_training}

Every architecture is trained with the same procedure.

Rather than using one fixed learning rate for the entire training, every architecture uses \texttt{StepLR} to cut the learning rate by a factor of ten every time a fixed, architecture-specific number of epochs (the step size) has passed (Section~\ref{sec:exp_hyperparams}), since large steps are useful early on to make fast progress but become counter-productive once the model is close to a good solution, where they cause it to overshoot and hinder convergence.

Training is allowed to run for up to 100 epochs, but is stopped early if development loss has not improved for 10 consecutive epochs, since the point at which a given architecture stops improving and starts overfitting is not known ahead of time and differs from one architecture to the next. Development \emph{loss}, rather than accuracy, is used to decide both when to stop and which epoch's weights to keep: whichever epoch achieved the lowest development loss is the one whose weights are restored and carried forward to testing. Figure~\ref{fig:training_flow} summarises the training process.

\section{Experiment 1: Mixed-Facility Baseline}
\label{sec:exp_mixed}

The first experiment does not take into account which facility an image comes from. The images of all five facilities are pooled and divided into training, development and test sets using the chronological split described in Chapter~\ref{chap:systemmethods}. Every image captured from January 2020 onwards is a test image. The images captured before January 2020 are grouped by facility, unit and class, each group is shuffled using the fixed random seed of 42, and the group is then divided in an 80/20 ratio into training and development images. A group with at least three images always receives at least one development image. Table~\ref{tab:stratified_split} gives the resulting number of images per class.

All five models are trained using this division of the data, for both the clarifiers and the aerobic zones, with the hyperparameter values in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} (Section~\ref{sec:exp_hyperparams}). 

Experiment~1 is also used to measure the effect of augmentation. Every model is trained a second time on the same data but without augmentation (resizing and normalisation is maintained for all train, development and test images). The data, hyperparameters, class weights and random seed are the same in both experiments, to ensure that augmentation is the only difference. In Experiment~1, 20 models are therefore trained: five models for each of the two components, each trained with and without augmentation. The results are given in Section~\ref{sec:results_augmentation}.

The purpose of this experiment is to estimate how well the models perform at facilities whose earlier images are part of the training data. This is the usual situation in the intended use of the system (Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}): new images of a facility are classified, an operator relabels the images of components classified as not working, and the relabelled images are added to the training data. The test images were all captured after the training and development images, as they would be in practice. Since a satellite image has a single capture date, no test image comes from the same satellite image as a training or development image.

A facility that is added to the system has no labelled images until its first images have been reviewed. A model could also learn cues that are specific to one facility, such as the colour of the water at that facility, and still perform well on this split, since the same cues are present in the test set. The results of Experiment~1 therefore do not show how a model would perform at a facility it has never seen, which is the reason for Experiment~2. The difference between the two experiments gives an indication of how much performance is lost when a model is applied to a facility it was not trained on.


\section{Experiment 2: Leave-One-Facility-Out Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp_lofo}

Experiment~2 uses the leave-one-facility-out split described in Chapter~\ref{chap:systemmethods}. One facility is held out as the test set, and the images of the remaining facilities are divided into training and development sets in an 80/20 ratio, grouped by facility, unit and class in the same way as in Experiment~1. This is repeated until every facility has been held out once. The images of the test facility are never used to update the weights of the network, to calculate the class weights, to decide when training stops or to select the checkpoint that is kept. The test results at a held-out facility are therefore an estimate of how a model would perform at a facility it has never seen.

Every architecture is trained separately for every held-out facility, using the hyperparameters (Section~\ref{sec:exp_hyperparams}), class-weighted loss (Section~\ref{sec:exp_loss}), augmentation (Section~\ref{sec:exp_augmentation}) and training loop (Section~\ref{sec:exp_training}) described earlier in this chapter, with the same hyperparameters used throughout. For the clarifiers, this gives five held-out facilities and 25 trained models (one for each of the five models and each held-out facility). Aerobic zones are only labelled at Atlantis, Cape Flats and Waterval (Chapter~\ref{chap:systemmethods}), so in this case only three facilities are held out, giving 15 trained models. Since Fisantekraal and Northern Works contribute no aerobic zone images, every aerobic zone model is trained on the two aerobic facilities that are not held out. In this experiment the training and test sets differ considerably in size between held-out facilities. Cape Flats contains 1476 of the 2527 clarifier images, so when it is held out, the model is trained on only 846 images, less than half of what the models are trained on when another facility is held out, and tested on the largest test set. Waterval plays a similar role for the aerobic zones, with 864 of the 1743 images. The class composition of the test sets also differs between facilities. The Atlantis test set contains no \textbf{Dysfunctional} clarifiers and only three \textbf{Empty} clarifiers, and the Waterval test set contains no \textbf{Empty} clarifiers and only seven \textbf{Dysfunctional} clarifiers, while \textbf{Dysfunctional} clarifiers make up almost a third of the Northern Works test set. The recall of a class cannot be calculated at a facility where that class does not occur, and a class with only a handful of test images at a facility leads to an uncertain estimate. For the same reason, the accuracy at one held-out facility cannot be compared directly with that at another: a model that recognises one class well and another poorly will score very differently depending on which class dominates the test set. Macro-averaged metrics, which give every class the same weight, are averaged only over the classes that occur in the held-out test set. Otherwise, a class without any test images would count as a score of zero, and would incorrectly lower the macro average of the Atlantis and Waterval clarifier test sets.

To summarise the performance of an architecture, the results per held-out facility are averaged, with every facility counting equally regardless of the size of its test set. The standard deviation across held-out facilities is reported alongside the mean. Pooling the test images of all held-out facilities (micro-averaging) would let Cape Flats determine most of the clarifier result. The standard deviation shows how much the performance of an architecture depends on the facility it is tested on.

One part of this experiment is not fully independent of the test facility. The hyperparameters were tuned once per architecture, with Northern Works held out for the clarifiers and Atlantis held out for the aerobic zones (Section~\ref{sec:exp_hyperparams}), and the training and development sets used for tuning contain images from the other facilities. For the other held-out facilities, their images were therefore part of the training or development set on which the hyperparameters were chosen, although they were never used to train the model that is tested on them. Since only six hyperparameters were chosen in this way, the effect is likely to be small, but only the Northern Works clarifier results and the Atlantis aerobic zone results are fully independent of the tuning.


\section{Experiment 3: Binary (OK / Not-OK) Reformulation}
\label{sec:exp_binary}

The multi-class models distinguish between several ways in which a component can fail, but for an operator the most important question is whether a component needs attention or not. A \textbf{Scum} clarifier classified as \textbf{Dysfunctional} would still be inspected by an operator, whereas a \textbf{Scum} clarifier classified as \textbf{Functional} would not be. Experiment~3 therefore evaluates the models as binary classifiers, which only decide whether a component is functioning correctly (OK) or not (Not-OK). For the clarifiers, \textbf{Functional} means OK, while \textbf{Dysfunctional}, \textbf{Empty} and \textbf{Scum} are Not-OK. For the aerobic zones, \textbf{Functional} means OK, while \textbf{Suboptimal} and \textbf{Dysfunctional} are Not-OK.

No separate binary models are trained. Instead, the binary decision is derived from the multi-class models of Experiments~1 and~2. The Not-OK score of an image is the total probability that the model assigns to the Not-OK classes,

\begin{equation}
    s = 1 - P(\mathrm{Functional}),
\end{equation}

and the image is classified as Not-OK when $s \geq t$, for a threshold $t$. The binary results therefore come from exactly the same models as the multi-class results, so the two can be compared directly. The binary evaluation is carried out on both the chronological split of Experiment~1 and the leave-one-facility-out split of Experiment~2.

With Not-OK treated as the positive class, the recall (or sensitivity) is the fraction of Not-OK components that are classified as Not-OK ($s \geq t$), while the specificity is the fraction of OK components that are classified as OK ($s < t$). These two kinds of mistake do not have the same cost. A false alarm only costs an operator the time to look at the image and make his or her own confirmatory assessment, while a missed Not-OK component may go unnoticed until the next satellite image of the facility becomes available. The decision threshold was therefore chosen so that the recall of the Not-OK class reaches a target of 95\% on the development set used to train the same model. The recall is evaluated at 500 evenly spaced thresholds between 0 and 1, and the largest threshold that still reaches the target is selected, since this is the threshold that also raises the fewest false alarms. The recall, specificity and precision are then calculated on the test set at this threshold. For the leave-one-facility-out models of Experiment~2, each model has its own threshold, chosen on its own development set, which contains no images of the held-out facility.

The ROC curve and its AUC are also calculated for the binary problem, since they describe the trade-off between recall and specificity over all thresholds rather than at a single one. For the leave-one-facility-out models, the binary results are summarised across held-out facilities in the same way as the multi-class results (Section~\ref{sec:exp_lofo}). The ROC curve of every held-out facility is interpolated onto a common set of false positive rates before the curves are averaged, and the binary confusion matrix at the chosen threshold is normalised per row, so that it shows rates rather than counts, before it is averaged.

Since the test set plays no part in choosing the threshold, the test recall can be lower or higher than 95\%. The development set is also used to select the checkpoint (Section~\ref{sec:exp_training}), so the development recall may be slightly optimistic. For the leave-one-facility-out split, the test recall is also reported for every held-out facility separately. In practice, the threshold would have to be fixed before the system is used at a new facility, so a test recall well below 95\% at some facilities would mean that a threshold chosen on known facilities cannot be relied on to reach the target recall at a new one.


\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:exp_metrics}

Every trained model is evaluated once on its test set, after training has finished and the checkpoint with the lowest development loss has been restored (Section~\ref{sec:exp_training}). A test image is assigned to the class with the highest softmax probability, and the metrics described in Section~\ref{sec:metrics_background} are calculated from these classifications and probabilities.

Of these metrics, recall is the most important in the context of this project. The system is meant to identify components that are not working properly, so that an operator can inspect them. A component with low recall for a failure class, such as a \textbf{Dysfunctional} clarifier or a \textbf{Suboptimal} aerobic zone, would be missed and could go unnoticed until the next satellite image of the facility, whereas a false positive only costs an operator the time to look at an image. For this reason, the recall of the failure classes is reported alongside the macro-averaged scores, and the operating point in Experiment~3 is chosen to reach a target recall rather than the highest accuracy.


\section{Qualitative Analysis: Grad-CAM}
\label{sec:exp_gradcam}

Classification performance alone does not show what in an image a model bases its decision on, so a model could rely on something unrelated to the state of the component, such as the water colour at a particular facility or the rim of a clarifier. Grad-CAM (Section~\ref{sec:gradcam_background}) is used to check this, by highlighting the regions of an image that contributed the most to a classification.

\subsection{Method}
\label{subsec:gradcam_method}

Grad-CAM was applied to the output of the last feature block of each selected model, using the \texttt{pytorch-grad-cam} library~\citep{gildenblat2021pytorchcam}. The heatmaps are superimposed on the input images, where red regions contributed the most to the classification. Every heatmap is normalised separately, so the colours cannot be compared between images.

\subsection{Models and Example Selection}
\label{subsec:gradcam_setup}

For each component, the architecture with the highest mean AUC on the test sets of the leave-one-facility-out experiment (Section~\ref{sec:exp_lofo}) was selected for analysis using one of the held-out test sets. For the clarifiers, Fisantekraal was used, since it contains all four clarifier classes, and for the aerobic zones, Waterval was used, since it has the most aerobic zone images. For each class, one correctly classified image was chosen at random, along with one image from each of the most common mistakes. The heatmaps are calculated for the assigned class, so for a mistake they show what the model took as evidence to arrive at its incorrect decision.
