\mychapter{Literature Review}{Literature Review}{}
\label{chap:literature}

This chapter reviews the work that focuses on one question: can the state of an aerobic zone or clarifier be judged reliably from historical satellite images, without a site visit? No single field answers it. The relevant studies are spread over four areas, namely image analysis inside treatment plants, satellite remote sensing of water quality, remote sensing of treatment plants, and the behaviour of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on small and uneven datasets.

\section{The Monitoring Problem in South Africa}
\label{sec:sa_monitoring}

The 2022 Green Drop report for the Department of Public Works shows a clear gap between official records and actual plant conditions. Out of 115 systems, 102 are in a critical state. The report notes a disconnect between administrative data and physical fieldwork. Physical inspections found differences of up to 59\% between paper-based scores and the real state of the facilities. This problem statement proposes using imagery to spot these differences, as the report proves that scores based on submitted paperwork can hide true performance.

\section{Reading Process State from Images Inside the Plant}
\label{sec:in_plant_imaging}

Researchers have long tried to turn the operator's visual judgement of activated sludge into measurement. Quantitative image analysis of microscope images of flocs and filaments grew into a field of its own \citep{costa2013}, but each application needs hand-tuned steps such as thresholding, and a practitioner must choose which algorithms and threshold values to use \citep{borzooei2024}. CNNs remove much of this work by learning features from labelled examples. \citet{satoh2021} trained binary CNN classifiers that recognised changes in floc morphology and the abundance of filamentous bacteria. \citet{borzooei2024} predicted the sludge volume index from microscope images taken weekly at a Belgian industrial plant over almost two years. Transfer learning from ImageNet generally beat training from scratch, the larger networks overfitted, and the small ConvNeXt-nano generalised best ($R^2 = 0.75$). Their model also underestimated the highest index values, which the authors traced to how few such samples the training set held.

A camera above the tank is the next step up in scale. \citet{carballomato2024} used images from a pan-tilt-zoom camera at a working plant to segment surface foam with texture segmentation models, reaching a Dice score of 86\%, and reported the percentage of the tank surface covered by foam to the operators in real time.

These studies agree that the appearance of sludge and tank surfaces carries real information about process state, and that CNNs can extract it from modest datasets. That supports the premise of this project. The studies by \citet{borzooei2024} and \citet{carballomato2024}, however, both need sampling or equipment at the plant, which is the cost the problem statement wants to avoid. \citet{borzooei2024} also trained and tested their models on samples from a single plant, so their results do not show whether a model can judge a plant it has not seen.

\section{Satellite Remote Sensing of Water Quality}
\label{sec:rs_water_quality}

Satellite studies of water quality focus on suspended solids and turbidity, and water clarity falls as the suspended solids concentration rises \citep{gholizadeh2016}. A clarifier that loses solids should therefore look brighter than one that retains them, which fits the rule that a working clarifier looks almost black. At close range a CNN can read suspended solids from ordinary colour photographs. \citet{lopezbetancur2022} fine-tuned a pretrained AlexNet on smartphone images of liquid samples and estimated total suspended solids with 98\% accuracy, although under controlled LED lighting.

The main challenge is image resolution. Current methods measure water quality by analyzing the light reflecting off the water, but they typically use satellites like Landsat \citep{gholizadeh2016}, which do not capture enough detail for small bodies of water. With pixels tens of metres wide, a clarifier covers only a few pixels and the surface pattern of an aeration zone vanishes. Google Earth imagery has the detail and is increasingly used for visual interpretation, but its coverage is uneven, and before the survey by \citet{lesiv2018} little was known about where such imagery exists or when it was captured. \citet{hu2013} found it almost as accurate as QuickBird imagery for land cover mapping, with its strength in classes recognisable by shape and context. This project is the opposite case. Every clarifier is round, every aeration zone is rectangular, and the classes differ only in surface appearance. The project therefore cannot compute a physical quantity such as suspended solids and must learn the visual categories that operators already use.

\section{Remote Sensing of Treatment Plants}
\label{sec:rs_treatment_plants}

In the remote sensing literature, treatment plants appear mainly as objects to be found. Wastewater treatment plant is one of 38 scene classes in the PatternNet benchmark, with one label per plant whatever its condition \citep{zhou2018patternnet}. \citet{li2022wwtp} combined OpenStreetMap labels with multimodal satellite data to detect plants and tested the method around Stuttgart. \citet{shuai2023} built an attention-based detector for Gaofen-2 imagery that located plants across Beijing with 80.1\% accuracy at 90.4\% recall, and noted that the mix of rectangular and circular tanks makes plants harder to detect with conventional methods. \citet{randhawa2023} worked towards detection worldwide and named the variation between satellite images as one of the main difficulties. In all of this work the tanks help to find a plant, and none of these studies asks whether the tanks are working. The closest regulatory precedent, from agriculture, has the same limit. \citet{handannader2019} applied a CNN to high-resolution imagery of North Carolina, found 589 more poultry feeding operations than a manual count had (an increase of 15\%), and argued that the method can inform compliance priorities. Their target was the location of a facility, not its behaviour.

The only study found in this review that used imagery to judge whether treatment plants were working is an undergraduate prize essay. \citet{westbrook2016} compared historical Google Earth images of six plants in and around Havana and concluded that none ran at full capacity. The essay shows that historical imagery holds evidence of plant condition, and it also shows the limits of reading that evidence by eye. Its judgements were coarse, such as whether a clarifier held water, they rested on one team's interpretation, and the authors noted that a proper assessment would need site visits. Between detection studies that automate the search but ignore performance, and a study that judges performance by hand, lies the task this project takes on.

\section{Pretrained CNNs on a Small, Texture-Defined Problem}
\label{sec:pretrained_cnns}

With little labelled data, training a CNN from scratch is usually not practical. \citet{nogueira2017} compared full training, fine-tuning and fixed feature extraction for six CNNs on three remote sensing datasets and found that fine-tuning tended to work best, a result that \citet{borzooei2024} repeat in a very different domain. This project therefore fine-tunes pretrained networks, and trains AlexNet from scratch only as a baseline that measures what pretraining is worth (Section~\ref{sec:exp_architectures}). The earlier study by \citet{penatti2015} adds a caution. ImageNet features gave the best results on aerial scenes but were beaten by a simple colour descriptor on multispectral scenes of coffee crops. Features learnt from everyday objects may therefore miss the cue when classes differ mainly in colour, as the clarifier classes do.

Texture is the other cue. \citet{geirhos2019} showed that ImageNet-trained CNNs rely on texture far more than people do, which they framed as a weakness for recognising objects. Here the bias may help, since a working aeration zone is identified by its surface texture, and \citet{carballomato2024} likewise treated tank foam as a texture problem. The bias cuts both ways, though. Concrete walls, walkways and the rotating arms of clarifiers also have strong textures that a model may learn in place of the water. The six pretrained models compared in this project come from five families published between 2016 and 2022 \citep{he2016resnet, szegedy2016inception, huang2017densenet, tan2019efficientnet, liu2022convnext}, and no comparison of these families on treatment plant imagery was found. Since \citet{borzooei2024} found that larger models did worse on limited data, the smaller variants deserve a fair trial.

Uneven classes add a further problem, because the rare states may be the ones the tool exists to find. \citet{buda2018} showed that class imbalance harms CNNs and that oversampling was the best remedy in almost all their tests. \citet{lin2017focal} proposed focal loss, which down-weights easy examples so that training focuses on hard ones. Both methods were developed on data with distinct classes, such as handwritten digits and everyday objects. The classes here are graded judgements, defined by thresholds such as a third or two thirds of the surface, so a hard example may be borderline or mislabelled rather than informative. Neither study examined that case. This project uses neither method, and instead weights the cross-entropy loss of each class inversely to its frequency in the training set (Section~\ref{sec:exp_loss}).

\section{Testing on Unseen Plants and Checking What the Model Sees}
\label{sec:generalisation}

\citet{kattenborn2022} demonstrated that how you select test data is critical in remote sensing. When analyzing drone imagery, choosing random test images made the model appear up to 28\% more accurate than it actually was. This happens because images taken from the same area and at the same time are too similar, giving the model an unfair advantage. To get a realistic performance score, the model must be tested on entirely separate geographic locations, a rule that becomes even more important when you only have a small amount of data. The data in this project share that structure in a stronger form. Each tank appears on many dates, so a random split would put images of the same tank, taken months apart, into both the training and test sets.

Inflated scores are not the only risk. \citet{geirhos2020} describe shortcut learning, in which a network finds a rule that works on the test set but fails under new conditions, and \citet{lapuschkin2019} give the well-known case of a classifier that recognised horses by a copyright tag common in horse photographs. With a handful of plants imaged over many years, the equivalent shortcut would be to recognise which plant is in view and how often that plant was labelled as failing. Holding out whole facilities blocks this route. Checking where the model looks is a second safeguard. Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) highlights the image regions that drive a CNN's prediction \citep{selvaraju2017gradcam}, so it can test what the problem statement actually asks: whether the model uses the same cues as an operator, and not merely whether it reaches the same answer.

What to measure is the last open question. Overall accuracy hides poor results on rare classes, which is why \citet{buda2018} used a multi-class version of the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. \citet{saito2015} argue that precision-recall curves are more informative than ROC curves when classes are imbalanced, so the ROC-based AUC used in this project is reported alongside the per-class precision and recall rather than on its own. Cost matters as well. A tool that triggers inspections can accept some false alarms but should rarely miss a failing unit, so recall on the failing classes, at a threshold chosen for that purpose, is the more useful measure here (Section~\ref{sec:exp_binary}).
